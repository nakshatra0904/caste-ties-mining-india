# Mining workers: caste composition, job entry and employment conditions

## What the additional evidence contributes

Worker composition is observable in several sources, even though caste-linked recruitment and firm growth remain incompletely observed. I added a national Census worker baseline, Coal India administrative employee snapshots, published recruitment flows and two Rajasthan stone-sector studies. They answer different questions. Census records industry-classified persons; Coal India records corporate employees; Rajasthan studies interview selected workers across extraction and processing. None supplies a linked national employer-worker network panel. I used actual published cells and retained their denominators, geographical coverage, dates and source locations. [S56] [S57] [S58] [S59] [S60] [S61] [S62]

The first distinction is between workers' own social identity and their employers' identity. EC6 employment classified by the proprietor's group does not reveal the group of the people employed. The B18 SC and ST tables directly classify workers by their own broad group. These series therefore substantially improve the labour-side description, but cannot be merged to EC6 to construct a worker-employer pairing. The second distinction is between a route into employment and the social identity of the person providing that route. A relative or colleague can provide a referral without sharing a caste; a same-category contact need not share a jati. Recruitment through relationships is evidence of relational hiring, rather than a direct measure of caste homophily.

## National Census extraction and population definitions

I used three official Census 2011 Excel workbooks: B18 all persons, B18SC and B18ST. I selected NIC 2008 divisions 05, 06, 07, 08 and 09, with group code 000 and class code 0000. This avoids counting a division and then counting its constituent groups and classes again. The five divisions cover coal/lignite, petroleum/natural gas, metal ores, other mining/quarrying and mining-support services. Historical state codes 01-35 are retained; code 00 is the separate India total and never enters the regression. [S56] [S57] [S58]

The national total is 2,105,736 main plus marginal workers in the selected divisions. SC workers number 439,519, or 20.87 percent, and ST workers number 364,749, or 17.32 percent. The remaining 1,301,468 workers, or 61.81 percent, are derived by subtraction. That remainder contains OBC and other workers; it is not an upper-caste population. The source does not provide an OBC worker table. I did not manufacture a population-wide OBC denominator from the remainder.

Main workers worked for at least six months during the preceding reference year; marginal workers worked for less than six months. This is an annual-duration distinction. It does not classify permanent versus temporary contracts, regular versus contractor payroll, skilled versus unskilled employment, or insured versus uninsured workers. It also includes industry-classified workers beyond wage employees. Thus a marginal-worker share is a defensible duration outcome, while calling it the informal-employment rate would be incorrect. The official definitions are also available at https://data.gov.in/catalog/main-workers-marginal-workers-non-workers-and-those-marginal-workers-non-workers .

I checked every extracted numeric column for male plus female equalling persons and rural plus urban equalling total. SC and ST counts must not exceed the corresponding all-worker cell when combined. Missing published zero division rows are padded only against the all-worker grid, and all state sums then reconcile to the national workbook entries. I applied these checks separately to main and marginal employment and retained original workbook row numbers. Published counts are not used as a substitute for access to individual Census records.

The worker total is much larger than the EC6 establishment employment total of 557,474. The series have different observation units, reference periods, industry classification procedures and enumeration coverage. This discrepancy must be investigated through the original schedules and producer clarification before any coverage adjustment. It cannot be interpreted as growth, a measured informal-work residual, or evidence that a specific fraction of miners was missed by EC6. Neither arithmetic difference nor a cross-source ratio creates a valid estimate of unregistered employment.

## Descriptive segmentation across subsectors, places and sex

SC and ST participation varies sharply across the five divisions. In metal-ore mining the ST share is 34.83 percent, compared with 13.46 percent in coal/lignite and 8.31 percent in petroleum/natural gas. SC shares are 23.14 percent in other mining/quarrying, 18.29 percent in coal/lignite and 11.72 percent in metal ores. These are industry-worker denominators, rather than shares of the SC or ST population who work in mining. The distinction matters: an industry composition statistic cannot establish an individual's probability of getting a mining job.

The unadjusted marginal-worker shares are 15.59 percent among SC workers, 22.74 percent among ST workers and 11.75 percent among the remainder. Coal/lignite has relatively low marginal shares; other mining/quarrying has substantially higher values across groups. The appended sex-specific graph and all-state table show why pooling mining as a single industry can conceal composition differences. State charts include small workforces, and therefore retain counts alongside percentages. A high group share in a small island or northeastern workforce should not be mistaken for a large contribution to national mining employment.

Three descriptive targets are kept separate: national pooled shares, state-level shares and the average across state cells. I used pooled counts for the first target, each state's own count for the second, and employment weights in my regressions. These choices answer different questions; an unweighted average over states would overweight very small mining workforces. No worker is represented by a reconstructed individual record. The full rural/urban-by-sex-by-division-by-group cells are available for further descriptive work.

## Controlled comparisons: four fitted models

I defined the added regression outcome as the percentage of workers in each observed cell who are marginal workers. Cells cross historical state, NIC division, male/female, rural/urban and SC/ST/remainder. Zero-employment cells have no defined percentage and are excluded from fitting. There are 1,275 nonempty cells in 35 historical state/UT regions, with denominator weights summing to 2,105,736. This is 1,275 aggregate observations, not 2.11 million independently observed survey records.

```math
y_c=100\frac{M_c}{N_c},\qquad
\underset{\theta}{\operatorname{minimize}}\ \sum_c N_c(y_c-X_c\theta)^2.
```

W1 includes SC and ST indicators with the remainder as the reference group. W2 adds sex and rural/urban residence. W3 adds historical state and NIC-division fixed effects. W4 instead absorbs the complete state-by-division-by-sex-by-residence stratum, comparing social groups within the same joint context. The weights are the cell's main-plus-marginal count. W4 is a more demanding descriptive comparison because additive controls alone cannot account for every interaction between place, mineral and labour-market composition.

```math
y_{g,s,d,k,a}=\alpha_{s,d,k,a}
 +\beta_{SC}\mathbf{1}\{g=SC\}
 +\beta_{ST}\mathbf{1}\{g=ST\}+u_{g,s,d,k,a}.
```

The unadjusted SC contrast is +3.83 percentage points, falling to +2.47 in W2, +1.89 in W3 and +1.49 in W4. The ST contrast falls from +10.99 to +7.58, +6.23 and +5.08 percentage points, respectively. Relative to the unadjusted contrasts, the W4 values are about 61 percent smaller for SC and 54 percent smaller for ST. This attenuation shows that the pooled differences are partly associated with where people work, the mining division and sex/residence composition. It is not a decomposition into causal contributions of those factors.

The W3 model-based 95 percent intervals are +0.35 to +3.43 points for SC and +5.16 to +7.30 for ST. W4 intervals are -0.46 to +3.45 and +3.58 to +6.58. Thus the more demanding within-stratum SC comparison is less conclusive, while the ST duration difference remains positive in these fitted specifications. All coefficients and controls are archived, including the absorbed stratum coefficients, and the regression table identifies the reference group and units. The four models form a stated sensitivity sequence; their overlapping intervals should not be read as independent replications.

I clustered model inference at the 35 historical states/UTs and used a finite-sample covariance correction and t reference distribution. These intervals describe a model across regional aggregates. They do not quantify Census enumeration errors, recover unavailable household survey design variance, or cure selection and omitted-variable bias. Thirty-five clusters give limited asymptotic reassurance; W4 uses many strata relative to the cell count, so its clustered intervals merit particular caution. The point estimates and their change with controls are the principal descriptive evidence. A policy or causal dissertation would need suitable design-based or small-cluster inference under its actual sampling/assignment scheme.

Age, education, occupation within the division, work experience, landholding, firm capital, contractor status and recruitment route are not jointly observed in these Census cells. I therefore did not add these unavailable controls. The remaining coefficients cannot be described as equal-productivity comparisons, discrimination effects or the effect of caste ties on hiring. Duration itself may depend on preferences, seasonality, secondary work and labour demand. Workers already employed in mining are selected from a wider population, so even a perfectly fitted employment-duration model cannot explain who gained access to mining employment in the first place.

## Formal coal employment and observable recruitment flows

Coal Ministry chapter 16 reports Coal India's employment groups A-D and SC/ST/OBC composition. On 1 January 2024 the disclosed total is 231,058 employees: 44,601 SC, 33,143 ST and 58,296 OBC. On 1 January 2026 the total is 214,333: 35,351 SC, 26,755 ST and 40,395 OBC. Recomputed 2026 shares are 16.49, 12.48 and 18.85 percent. These are corporate employee stocks, including roles beyond mine-face work, and do not represent all Indian miners or the full contractor workforce. The 2026 residual includes the separately disclosed 510 EWS employees. Employment groups A-D are retained as administrative labels rather than automatically renamed as occupations. [S59] [S60]

The difference between snapshots is not a recruitment effect. Explaining a change in a group's stock requires opening stock plus hires and transfers minus retirements, deaths and other exits, together with consistent coverage and classification. Those matched flows are absent. In addition, published recruitment reservation percentages describe eligibility rules for vacancies, not realised workforce shares. A disability table in the same chapter has the same total employee denominator but reports only employees with benchmark disabilities; it is deliberately excluded from the caste-workforce calculation.

An observable recruitment example is the SECL Mining Sirdar disclosure for FY2024-25: 350 advertised vacancies and 345 filled. The ST vacancy category has 97 advertised and 92 filled, with five non-joiners reported; UR, SC, EWS and OBC category counts fill their disclosed advertised numbers. The resulting 94.85 percent ST vacancy-fill rate has advertised reserved vacancies as its denominator. It is not the probability an ST applicant was hired. UR describes an unreserved vacancy category and cannot be relabelled as a general-caste employee identity. Recruitment through advertised examinations, GATE/CBT for specified management-trainee streams, and separately reported dependent-employment arrangements should be measured as distinct routes. [S60]

This distinction between public recruitment institutions and informal referral chains is analytically useful. It suggests separate dissertation strata rather than assuming a single Indian mining labour market. A comparison of company payroll groups with quarry interviewees can motivate questions about institutional access, but requires sampling and common outcomes before numerical gaps between those sources can be interpreted.

## Rajasthan stone workers and job-entry evidence

Arisa/CLRA's 2024 report covers 97 individual interviews: 47 in Budhpura and 50 in Makrana; ten Budhpura home-workers used a different instrument. Published SC/ST/OBC shares are 36/19/45 percent in Budhpura and 82/0/18 percent in Makrana. The Budhpura composition paragraph leaves the precise instrument denominator unresolved. Of Makrana's 50 workers, 80 percent entered through relatives/co-workers, seven approached an employer and three used contractors. These are local, non-probability supply-chain samples, not nationally representative miners. No caste-by-route cross-tabulation is published. [S61]

Additional Makrana counts include 20 illiterate workers, seven with secondary education or higher, 48 local residents, 20 PPE users and no written employment contracts. An advance paragraph reports 18/50, while the conclusion reports 38 percent; the discrepancy is retained rather than silently corrected. Budhpura's main instrument reports 31/37 piece-rate workers. The report's wage averages are not used to reconstruct individual pay or an adjusted caste wage gap. [S61]

The ILO's 2021 Rajasthan study interviewed 115 workers and 43 owners, largely in the formal value chain across two clusters. Worker composition is reported as 34 percent SC, 30 ST, 30 OBC and 5 General; the 99-percent sum reflects rounded publication values and is not forced to 100. It describes informal connections and direct approaches as hiring routes. Other published indicators cover literacy, financial inclusion, local residence and Provident Fund access. Reported annual work duration differs by sex and production stage. Individual records and caste-by-recruitment tables were not obtained. [S62]

These observations motivate specific fieldwork questions. The recruitment agent, legal employer, wage payer and current supervisor may be different people. Being local does not imply recruitment without relationships, and a contractor is not necessarily the person who first supplied job information. An advance may affect the ability to leave a job, while the original referral may affect access to one. Each event needs its own date and relationship code. Safety equipment use also differs from effective protection; a survey should measure task, exposure, provision, training and use separately, without inferring disease from an interview response.

## Additional usable sources and the controls they would permit

The acquired PLFS 2023-24 layout and interview instructions verify household social group and person-level industry, occupation, enterprise type, enterprise workforce band, contract and earnings fields. The first-visit layout uses sg at household level, ind_pas and ind_sas for usual principal/subsidiary industry, and job_pas/job_sas for job-contract measures. Records must be joined using the full survey household identifier and visit/quarter context, rather than row order. Documentation is acquired; the unit records are not. No PLFS wage regression is claimed. [S11] [S63]

A defensible next wage analysis would define wage-employed miners under one activity-status convention, separate monthly regular earnings from daily casual earnings, and estimate log earnings against group indicators, age and age squared, education, sex, state, rural/urban residence and occupation. Hours, days and tenure should be added only when genuinely measured for the same earnings period. Industry and establishment characteristics can enter subsequent specifications to reveal how the descriptive gap changes. Report weighted effective sample sizes by group and mining division before fitting; a small ST oil-sector cell may not sustain a precise estimate. Survey strata, PSUs, multipliers and annualization instructions must be applied from the exact release.

Occupational controls may themselves reflect the labour-market channel under study. Consequently, an occupation-adjusted pay contrast answers a different question from the total pay difference. The same is true of current contractor status, advances and job tenure if recruitment affects them. A controlled coefficient should therefore be accompanied by an estimand and a causal diagram, rather than a claim that adding every available variable always improves identification. Separate baseline determinants from possible mediators and report the sequence of specifications.

The Labour Bureau's Occupational Wage Survey offers occupation-level pay and employment information for selected mining industries; the identified seventh-round four-mining-industry report is a specific acquisition lead. Its caste field and unit-record access have not been verified. Separate Labour Bureau SC/ST working-and-living-condition, unorganised-worker and women-worker survey families should be searched by industry, locality and vintage, rather than presumed to contain a modern national mining panel. Company reservation returns, union records, contractor wage registers and consented site rosters can complement these sources but will have distinct coverage and access rules. [S64] [S65]

## Matched worker-firm fieldwork and identified extensions

The additional W01-W30 instrument links a worker's broad self-reported group, job and recruitment event to an anonymous establishment identifier. It separately records who supplied information, who recommended the worker, who selected them, who pays wages, and whether a contact relationship existed before the vacancy. Optional jati and self-reported shared-community relationships require consent and declined/unknown codes; they are never inferred from surnames. Employer rosters should be reconciled with workers' accounts, including off-roster contractors and home workers where these fall inside the chosen supply-chain scope.

Sample workers through both site listing and area/household listing, with probabilities recorded. Employer-supplied lists alone may exclude mobile, indebted, dismissed or informal workers. Track incumbents, new recruits, applicants and rejected applicants separately. For hiring access, the relevant denominator is the eligible applicant/opportunity population; for retention it is the baseline recruited cohort; for productivity it is the matched firm over repeated periods. Recording those populations makes it possible to distinguish caste-related access from sorting among people already employed.

A future descriptive recruitment regression can use referral route as an outcome and pre-job age, education, prior experience, origin and recruitment cohort as controls, with firm or site effects where overlap permits. It needs observed joint records. I cannot identify a referral advantage from the published 80-percent Makrana referral share and 82-percent SC share: many joint distributions are compatible with those two margins. An individual-route regression reconstructed from them would merely encode arbitrary assumptions.

For causal work, an explicitly randomized job-information or introduction offer could estimate whether widening information access changes applications, interview invitations, hiring and retention, with owner and worker category heterogeneity specified before analysis. A trial manipulating information should be described as an access intervention; it does not randomize caste. Any paired employer-worker design must protect workers from disclosure to employers and monitor interference, job displacement and substitution between applicants. Firm sales and employment follow-ups are then required to test whether recruitment and retention channels affect growth. My controlled Census results establish a labour-duration baseline and sharpen hypotheses; they do not substitute for that design.
